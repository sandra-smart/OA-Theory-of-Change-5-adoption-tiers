Build Your Foundation
Organizational Preparation
The organization hasn't started aligning its team with OA. There's no formal structure for who does what and no connections to employer partners.
The organization is interested and starting to learn. They're thinking about team structure and exploring OA training like the Fellows leadership program.
The team is taking shape. Core job functions are covered, at least one staff member is going through OA leadership training and the organization has started building relationships with local employers.
The team is organized by function — each person has a clear role. At least half of participant-facing staff are certified. Formal employer partnerships are in place and key data is tracked monthly.
Every role is clearly defined. All participant-facing staff are certified. Multiple staff have completed OA leadership training. Technology coordinates referrals, training is co-created with employers and outcomes are tracked for a full year after placement.
🔍
Meet People Where They Are
Phase 1: Assess and Plan
There's no consistent process for learning about the people who walk through the door. Assessments aren't used and there's no standard way to understand someone's needs.
A basic intake process exists, but it's not standardized. Some assessments are used, but not for everyone. Orientation sessions may be offered, but attendance isn't tracked.
The organization collects key information about each person's background and needs. Everyone completes at least one assessment. An orientation introduces them to available services.
Structured intake captures work history, education and life circumstances. Assessments cover career interests, basic skills and digital abilities. Everyone gets an orientation and works with staff to create a personalized career and financial plan with measurable goals.
Comprehensive intake helps staff design the right path for each person. Everyone completes a full assessment set, including a nationally recognized digital skills evaluation. Plans include career goals, financial stability goals and a system for tracking progress over time.
📚
Deliver the Right Services
Phase 2: Equip and Connect
There are few or no structured services. Support is informal and varies from person to person, with no consistent approach.
Some support services are available — like help with resumes or referrals to community resources — but they're limited and not consistently offered to everyone.
Real support is tailored to each person — housing, transportation or childcare barriers are addressed. Skills training and some job-specific programs are offered.
Support is coordinated around what each person needs. Financial education, employer-aligned training and career exploration are offered. Life barriers — housing, childcare, transportation — are actively addressed.
The full support system is in place and technology-connected. Training follows proven curricula, co-designed with employers to match living-wage jobs. Every barrier — from digital skills gaps to housing instability — is addressed through a coordinated partner network.
🚀
Launch Careers, Stay Connected
Phase 3: Launch and Evaluate
There's no structured help with finding a job and no follow-up after someone is placed. The organization doesn't track what happens after people leave.
Some job placement help is available, but follow-up after someone starts working is inconsistent. Basic outcome information may be gathered, but not systematically.
The organization helps people find jobs through a documented process. They're beginning to check in with people after placement to see how things are going and catch problems early.
The organization connects people to employers and helps them land jobs. Staff check in at 1, 2, 3 and 6 months after placement — stepping in with support when needed. Key outcomes are tracked monthly.
People are placed in living-wage jobs. Staff follow up for a full year — at 1, 2, 3, 6 and 12 months. A formal alumni program keeps people connected to Goodwill long after completing the program. Detailed outcomes drive continuous improvement.
💡
Important: Most organizations will be at different stages for different rows — and that's completely normal. For example, an organization might be strong in job placement but still developing its assessment process. Each of the 25 service areas that make up OA moves at its own pace. Who Owns What — The 8 Roles That Make OA Work

Every one of the 25 service areas has a named owner — someone who is directly responsible for making it happen. Here are the eight roles and what they do. Smaller organizations may have one person covering multiple roles; what matters is that every area has someone accountable.
Mission Leader
★ 2 primary areas
Makes the big decisions — staffing structure and OA strategy
Program Manager
★ 2 primary areas
Keeps everything running — oversees operations across all 25 service areas
Career Navigator
★ 9 primary areas
Works directly with people every day — the most hands-on role and the heaviest workload
Business Engagement
★ 3 primary areas
Builds relationships with employers to create job opportunities and training partnerships
Community Engagement
★ 1 primary area
Connects with community organizations for outreach, referrals and support services
Program Coordinator
★ 3 primary areas
Manages orientation sessions, career exploration activities and alumni programs
Trainer
★ 4 primary areas
Teaches job skills, digital skills, financial literacy and workplace readiness
Data Specialist
★ 2 primary areas
Tracks what's working — manages outcome data, reporting and referral technology
